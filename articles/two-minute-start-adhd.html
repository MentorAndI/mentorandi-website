The two-minute start: how to begin when the task feels enormous

One of the most repeated pieces of advice in productivity, with an origin most people misremember — and a failure mode worth knowing before you rely on it.

From ADHD Mentor·7 min read

Let me be straight about the provenance first, because a lot of what you read about this technique presents it as though it came out of a laboratory.

It did not. The two-minute rule comes from Getting Things Done, David Allen's 2001 book on personal organisation. It is a piece of practitioner wisdom — derived from years of watching people work, not from a controlled trial. That does not make it wrong. It does mean anyone citing a specific percentage improvement is inventing it.

What is worth understanding is that Allen's rule and the thing most people call "the two-minute rule" are two different techniques, and confusing them is why it stops working.

Two rules wearing one name

Allen's original is a filing decision, not a motivational one. When you are processing a pile of incoming things, if an item would take less than about two minutes to deal with, do it immediately, because tracking it costs more than doing it. It is a rule about administrative overhead.

The version that has spread is different: when a task feels too big to begin, commit to doing just two minutes of it, on the theory that starting is the hard part and momentum takes over from there.

That second version is the useful one here, and it deserves a more careful name. Call it the two-minute start, because the two minutes is not the task. It is the entry.

Why entry is the expensive part

If you have read the piece on why this is not a willpower problem, this will sound familiar. The difficulty tends to sit in the transition — in getting from not-doing to doing — rather than in the doing itself. Once you are three minutes into a task, it is frequently fine. The awful part was the forty minutes beforehand, spent in the same room as the task, not doing it.

A two-minute start attacks exactly that transition. It works by making the commitment small enough that the usual objections have nothing to grip. You cannot reasonably argue that you do not have two minutes. You cannot claim you are not in the right headspace for two minutes. The argument you normally have with yourself simply has no premises available.

The point is not that two minutes of work is valuable. It is that two minutes is too small a thing to be worth refusing.

The evidence that is actually there

The two-minute framing has no direct trial behind it. What does have evidence is the underlying move: converting a vague intention into a specific, pre-decided action tied to a moment.

In the research literature this is called an implementation intention — an if-then plan. Gollwitzer and Sheeran's meta-analysis pooled ninety-four independent tests across more than eight thousand participants and found a medium-to-large effect on goal attainment. And in work looking specifically at ADHD, Gawrilow and Gollwitzer found that children with ADHD given an if-then plan inhibited an unwanted response as well as children without ADHD did, where the same goal held as a general intention did not produce that.

So the defensible claim is not "two minutes is magic". It is: pre-deciding a specific small action, attached to a specific moment, reliably outperforms holding a general intention. The two-minute start is one convenient shape for doing that.

How to run it properly

Most of the failures come from three mistakes.

Name a physical action, not a subject. "Two minutes on the report" is still a topic, and topics are exactly what your brain slides off. "Open the file and write one bad sentence" is a body movement. If you cannot picture yourself doing it, it is not specific enough yet.

Give yourself genuine permission to stop. This is the part people cheat on, and cheating breaks it. If the two minutes is secretly a trick to get you to do an hour, you will know, and by the third attempt the offer has lost its credibility. Sometimes you must actually stop at two minutes and count it as a success — otherwise you are not lowering the cost of entry, you are lying about it, and the resistance will price that in.

Attach it to a cue. A two-minute start you have to remember to deploy is just another intention. Tie it to something already in the day — after the first coffee, when the laptop opens, when you sit back down after lunch. That is the externalising move, and it is what turns a good idea into something that actually happens on a Wednesday.

Where it does not work

It is worth being honest about the limits, because being sold a universal tool and then finding it fails is its own small demoralisation.

It is poor for tasks with a long spin-up cost — anything where two minutes gets you through opening the software and nothing else. For those, the useful entry is the setup itself: the two minutes is spent getting everything open and ready, and the actual work is a separate, later commitment.

It is poor when the resistance is not about size. If you are avoiding an email because of what it says rather than because it is a lot of work, shrinking the task does not touch the reason. That is a different conversation, and the honest first step is usually naming what you are actually avoiding.

And it is not a plan. A two-minute start gets you across the threshold; it does not tell you what the week looks like. Used alone it can produce a lot of beginnings and very few finishes — which is why it belongs alongside a structure that lives outside your head, rather than as a replacement for one.

This is everyday executive-function support — task initiation and follow-through. It is not diagnostic and not clinical advice.
