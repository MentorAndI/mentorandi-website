You cannot choose where the fat comes off

The experiment has been run in the neatest possible way: train one limb, leave the other alone, and see what happens. Across thirteen studies, the answer is nothing.

From Health & Fitness Mentor·7 min read

Almost everyone starts here. There is a specific part of you that you would like to be smaller, and there is an exercise that appears to target it, so you do the exercise.

It is an entirely reasonable assumption. Muscles need energy, fat is stored energy, the fat is sitting right there on top of the muscle. Why would the body not take it from the nearest available store?

It does not, and the way this has been tested is unusually clean.

The one-limb experiment

The elegant design here is to use a person as their own control. Train one arm or one leg intensively for weeks, leave the other completely alone, then measure the fat on both. Same person, same diet, same hormones, same everything — the only difference is which limb did the work.

If spot reduction happened, the trained limb would lose more fat. It is about as direct a test as exercise science gets.

A systematic review and meta-analysis pooled thirteen such studies covering more than eleven hundred people. The pooled effect was essentially zero — a figure of −0.03, which is a statistician's way of saying nothing happened.

Same body, same diet. One limb trained for weeks, the other idle. The fat came off both — or neither.

And the sit-ups study

The abdominal version has been done too, and it is the one worth quoting to anyone still doing crunches for their stomach.

Vispute and colleagues had participants perform seven abdominal exercises, five days a week, for six weeks, while keeping diet constant. At the end: no change in abdominal fat, no change in abdominal circumference, no change in overall body fat.

Six weeks of dedicated core work, and the tape measure had not moved.

One honest complication

We should mention that this is not a completely closed file. A recent randomised controlled trial has argued that a particular protocol — abdominal endurance work rather than strength work — did produce localised loss, and its authors present it as evidence that spot reduction exists.

Hold that lightly. One trial arguing for an effect sits against a meta-analysis of thirteen studies finding none, and the sensible response to a single surprising result is to wait and see whether anyone else can reproduce it. We mention it because you may encounter it, and because you should hear about the exception from us rather than discover we left it out.

The practical advice does not change either way.

What the exercise is actually doing

This is the part the debunking usually skips, and it matters, because "sit-ups don't burn belly fat" gets heard as "sit-ups are pointless".

They are not. Training a muscle builds and maintains that muscle. What changes the shape of an area is the combination of the muscle underneath and the fat on top — and you have direct control over the first one.

Where the fat comes off, and in what order, is largely down to genetics, sex and hormones. Most people have one area that goes last, and it is usually the area they care most about. That is annoying, and it is not a failure of your training.

What to do instead

Separate the two goals. Losing fat is a whole-body process driven mainly by energy balance over time. Building shape is local and driven by training. Trying to achieve the first with the tools of the second is the actual error.

Train the muscle you want to see. Not to burn the fat above it, but because a developed muscle is what makes the area look different once the fat does come off. That is a slow build, and it is achievable without a gym.

Stop judging progress by the mirror in one spot. If you are losing fat, you are losing it everywhere, including the place you are watching — just more slowly there, and often last. Measuring only your stubborn area is the fastest way to conclude that nothing is working while something is.

And be patient with the last area. It is genuinely last for most people. That is biology, not evidence that you need a different exercise.

Why the myth is so durable

Because an enormous amount of money depends on it. Almost every ab gadget, waist trainer and "targeted" programme is selling this specific belief, and it is a belief people very much want to be true.

The version that is true is less marketable and considerably more freeing: you do not have to find the right exercise for your particular problem area. There isn't one. Which means you can stop searching and train the whole thing.

This is general information about everyday exercise, not medical or dietary advice, and an AI mentor does not diagnose or treat anything. Before starting or significantly changing how you train — particularly if you have an injury, a heart or joint condition, are pregnant, or are taking medication — please speak to your GP or a qualified professional. If your relationship with food, weight or your body feels distressing or controlling, that deserves proper support: speak to your doctor or an eating disorder service.
