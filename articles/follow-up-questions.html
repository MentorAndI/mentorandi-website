The follow-up question is the whole skill

Of everything in the charisma literature, this is the technique with the cleanest evidence and the lowest cost. It is also the one people are most reluctant to use.

From Charisma Mentor·8 min read

Think of the last person you met who left you thinking, unexpectedly, that the conversation had gone well.

There is a good chance they did not say anything memorable. There is a very good chance that what they did was keep asking about you — and that when you gave a short answer, they asked about that answer rather than moving on.

That specific move has been studied, and it does more work than almost anything else you could practise.

What the research found

Huang, Yeomans, Brooks, Minson and Gino ran two experiments in which strangers got acquainted, plus an observational study of heterosexual speed daters.

People who asked more questions were liked more. When participants were instructed to ask more questions, their partners rated them higher on responsiveness — a measure that bundles listening, understanding, validation and care — and that responsiveness was what drove the liking.

The important refinement is which questions counted. Not questions in general: follow-up questions, the ones that pick up what the other person just said and ask for more of it. Those are the ones that signal you were actually listening, because they could not have been prepared in advance.

And in the speed-dating data, this showed up where it is hardest to argue with: people who asked more questions were more likely to be asked on a second date.

A follow-up question is the only kind you cannot fake. It is proof of attention, delivered in about four words.

An honest note on the evidence

This is a well-regarded finding, and it has had normal scientific scrutiny. Kluger and Malloy published a critique of the statistical analysis in the third study, and the authors responded. The core result — that asking questions, especially follow-ups, increases liking — has held up across the work rather than resting on any single analysis.

We mention it because we would rather you heard it here than discovered later that the picture was more textured than an article implied.

Why people resist doing it

Knowing this changes almost nothing on its own, because the barrier is not knowledge. Three reasons people don't ask.

They are composing. The most common reason a follow-up doesn't happen is that you were not listening — you were assembling your own next contribution while the other person was still talking. You cannot ask about something you did not hear.

They think questions look uninformed. Particularly at work, particularly in a first meeting, there is a belief that asking makes you look like the junior person in the room. The evidence points the other way: it makes you look responsive, and responsiveness reads as confidence rather than deference.

They are waiting for their turn to relate. Someone says they have just moved house, and the reflex is "oh, we moved last year, it was chaos." That is a switch: it looks like engagement, and it moves the spotlight. Done repeatedly, it is the single most common way people accidentally make conversations about themselves.

The drill

Here is the exercise. It takes one conversation and no preparation.

Aim for three follow-ups. Not more questions overall — three moments where you take what they just said and ask for the next layer. "What made you decide that?" "How did that go in the end?" "What was the hardest part?" Four words is plenty.

Notice the switch urge and let one pass. When the impulse arrives to tell your parallel story, notice it and ask instead. You are not banned from talking about yourself — you are practising catching the reflex, once, so that later you can choose.

Ask about the choice, not the fact. "Where do you work?" is admin. "How did you end up there?" is a conversation. People are considerably more interesting on their decisions than on their circumstances.

Then check it afterwards. Two questions when you get home: could I say what mattered most to them? And did they ask me anything back? If the second answer is no every time, the problem is probably not your questions — it is that you have been talking to someone who is not interested, which is useful information of a different kind.

Where it goes wrong

Interrogation. Questions without any disclosure of your own becomes an interview, and people leave feeling they gave more than they got. The fix is not fewer questions; it is offering something of yourself between them. A short piece of self-disclosure earns the next question.

Questions with an answer already inside them. "Don't you think that was a mistake?" is not a question. Everyone can tell.

Using it as a hiding place. For anyone who finds talking about themselves uncomfortable, asking questions is a wonderfully socially acceptable way to never be seen. It works, it is pleasant for the other person, and it can quietly become a way of avoiding the harder skill of saying something real yourself.

Why this one first

If you are going to train one thing, train this. It is invisible when done well, it costs nothing, it works in interviews, dates, meetings and family dinners alike, and it has better evidence behind it than almost anything else marketed as charisma.

And it has an unexpected side effect. It is very difficult to feel self-conscious while you are genuinely curious about someone else, because the attention is pointed outwards. A lot of people come looking for confidence and find that curiosity did the job instead.
