Good enough is a standard, not an excuse

The phrase gets used as permission to stop trying. In the original idea it means something far more interesting: your ordinary, unavoidable failures are not a compromise. They are the mechanism.

From Parenting Mentor·8 min read

Most parents encounter "good enough" as a consolation. Someone says it kindly at the end of a bad week, and it lands as a lowered bar — you are not managing the real standard, so here is a smaller one you can reach.

That is not what it meant. And the actual meaning is far more useful, because it removes the standard you were failing rather than shrinking it.

Where the phrase comes from

The paediatrician and psychoanalyst Donald Winnicott introduced the good-enough mother in the early 1950s, and the structure of his idea is usually lost in the retelling.

His account starts with near-total adaptation. With a newborn, the parent very nearly meets every need as it arrives, which is what a newborn requires. Then — and this is the part that matters — the parent begins, inevitably, to fail. Not deliberately. They are tired, another child needs something, the phone rings, they misread the cry.

Winnicott's point was that this gradual, ordinary failing is not damage. It is how the child develops the capacity to manage. A parent who somehow adapted perfectly forever would leave the child with nothing to grow into. The frustration, arriving in tolerable doses against a background of reliable care, is the developmental engine.

The failures were never the price of good-enough parenting. They were the active ingredient.

Which reframes the whole thing. When you snap at bedtime and feel the guilt arrive, you are not falling short of the standard. You are, assuming the rest is reasonably reliable, doing the thing.

What the pursuit of perfect actually costs

This is not just a nicer way to feel about a hard job. Trying to be the parent who never fails has a documented cost, and it has been studied specifically.

Isabelle Roskam, Moïra Mikolajczak and colleagues have spent years establishing parental burnout as a distinct phenomenon, with its own measure. It has three dimensions: exhaustion in the parental role, emotional distancing from your own children, and a loss of the sense that you are any good at it.

The important finding for anyone tempted to file this under ordinary tiredness: in work with thousands of participants, parental burnout was shown to be distinct from job burnout and from depressive symptoms. It is not simply being worn out, and not simply being low.

It also has its own consequences. Alongside sleep and health problems and increased conflict in the couple, the research identifies escape ideation — the recurring thought of getting away from it entirely — and, at the more serious end, an increase in neglectful and violent behaviour towards the children. On that last measure, the effect of parental burnout is considerably larger than that of job burnout.

Read that in the right direction. This is not an argument that stressed parents are dangerous. It is an argument that parental exhaustion is not a private, cosmetic problem to be endured quietly — it has consequences for the children too, which means looking after yourself is part of the job rather than a distraction from it.

Guilt, and what to do with it

Guilt is not the enemy here, and telling parents to stop feeling it rarely works.

Guilt about a specific thing you did is useful information: it points at an action and suggests a repair. You were short with them, you go back and say so. That is a functional loop and it teaches something valuable — that people who love you can get it wrong and come back.

What is not useful is the diffuse version: the constant background sense of not being enough, attached to no particular act, and therefore repairable by nothing. That one does not improve your parenting. It just consumes the energy you needed for it, and it is the sort of thing that keeps the exhaustion dimension running.

A rough test: can you name the thing? If yes, go and repair it. If you cannot — if it is simply a hum — then it is not telling you anything about your child. It is telling you about your reserves.

What good enough looks like in practice

Reliable, not flawless. The background matters more than any episode. A child who is generally responded to can absorb a badly handled Tuesday without it meaning anything.

Repair beats prevention. You cannot avoid every rupture, and the attempt is what exhausts people. Going back afterwards — "I shouldn't have shouted, that wasn't about you" — does more work than never shouting would, and it is achievable. It is the same mechanism as the repair attempt between adults.

Treat your own capacity as part of the equipment. Given what the burnout research shows about the consequences, resting is not self-indulgence competing with your child's interests. It is maintenance of the thing your child depends on.

Cut the comparison inputs. Much modern parental guilt is manufactured — by an environment in which everyone else's parenting is visible in its edited form. That is not evidence about you.

And notice the three signs. Exhaustion specifically in the parental role, finding yourself emotionally distant from your children, and a collapsed sense of competence. Those are the measured dimensions, and if you recognise all three in yourself, this is worth taking to a professional rather than absorbing alone.

The reframe worth keeping

You are not aiming at a perfect childhood, and it is a good thing, because the perfect version would not produce a person who can cope with anything.

What you are aiming at is reliable enough, warm enough, and present enough — with the ordinary failures included, not eliminated. That is not a lowered bar for people who are struggling. On the original account, it is the bar.

This is general reflection on everyday family life. It is not therapy, not clinical advice and not a diagnostic tool. An AI mentor does not diagnose or treat parental burnout, depression or any other condition. If you recognise persistent exhaustion, emotional distance from your children or a collapsed sense of competence — and particularly if you have recurring thoughts of escaping, of harming yourself, or of harming your children — please speak to your GP or a qualified professional now. This is common, it is treatable, and telling someone is not an admission of failure. If you are in crisis, contact your local emergency services or a crisis line in your country.
