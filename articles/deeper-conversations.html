Small talk is harder than the deep version. Here's the evidence.

Everyone dreads the meaningful conversation with someone they barely know. Researchers had people try it anyway, and the dread turned out to be the only difficult part.

From Charisma Mentor·7 min read

Consider the standing arrangement most of us have with strangers. Weather. Traffic. How busy things are at the moment. Where you're from, what you do, how long the journey took.

It is exhausting, it produces nothing, and almost nobody enjoys it — yet we return to it with total reliability, because the alternative feels dangerous. Asking a colleague what they are actually worried about, or a new acquaintance what they would change about their life, feels like a social violation waiting to happen.

That intuition has been tested. It is wrong, and the way it is wrong is quite specific.

What the research found

Kardas, Kumar and Epley published a series of studies in the Journal of Personality and Social Psychology in 2022, under the title Overly shallow?

Their proposal was that people systematically underestimate how much distant strangers care about, and are interested in, their more intimate revelations — and that these miscalibrated expectations are themselves the barrier to deeper conversation.

In other words: it is not that deep conversations are risky. It is that we predict they will be, and that prediction determines which topics we choose. We select shallow subjects to avoid an awkwardness that mostly does not arrive.

The researchers also found the practical implication that matters most here: when expectations were better calibrated — when people had a more accurate sense of how the other person would respond — they chose to have deeper conversations.

The barrier is not the conversation. It is the forecast you make about the conversation, which you never get to check because you avoid the experiment.

Why the forecast is wrong

A few things conspire.

You know exactly how exposed you would feel saying the deeper thing, because you can simulate your own discomfort in vivid detail. You cannot simulate their interest nearly as well, so it gets underweighted.

There is also a mistake about what is being asked. "Deeper" sounds like confession — the difficult secret, the thing you would tell a therapist. In practice, the step down from small talk is much smaller than that: what someone is currently excited about, what they nearly did instead of this, what they have changed their mind about. None of that is a confession. All of it is a real conversation.

And there is a self-fulfilling structure. Shallow topics generate shallow responses, which supply no evidence that anything else would have been welcome. You leave with the belief intact.

The drill

Go one step down, not five. The move is not "what's your greatest fear". It is replacing one admin question with one real one. Not "how was your weekend" but "what's the best thing that happened this week". Not "what do you do" but "what are you working on that you actually find interesting". One step, once.

Go first. Depth is reciprocal and someone has to open. Offer a genuine sentence about yourself — mildly more real than the setting requires — and then ask. Asking someone to be open while you stay behind glass rarely works.

Choose the moment where it costs least. A taxi, a queue, the walk to the car park after a meeting. Situations you are leaving anyway carry almost no social risk, which makes them ideal practice ground.

Then record the actual result. This is the part that changes the forecast. Afterwards, write one line: what did I expect, and what happened? The expectation only gets corrected by evidence that you collected yourself. Reading that other people were wrong about this will not do it.

And notice the escape hatch. If you find yourself asking a deep question and then filling the silence before they can answer, you have not gone deeper — you have added a new topic to small talk. Let the pause sit. This is where the follow-up question does most of its work.

Where it does not apply

Some contexts genuinely want a light register: a professional first meeting where the other person is under time pressure, a room where hierarchy makes disclosure unequal, or anyone who has signalled that they would rather keep things brief. Reading that correctly is a skill in itself, and pushing through it is not depth, it is not listening.

The finding is also about topic choice in ordinary conversation, not a licence to interrogate strangers about their worst year.

The reason this one is worth practising

Because small talk does not scale into anything. You can be flawless at it for a decade and end up with a large number of people who know where you work.

Nearly every relationship you value started with somebody taking a small, slightly uncomfortable step past the script — and, if this research is right, feeling more nervous about it than they needed to. It is the same for the person opposite you, which means the step is usually being waited for from both sides at once.

This is general guidance on everyday conversation. An AI mentor teaches presence, voice and conversation — it is not a therapist and does not diagnose or treat social anxiety or any other condition. If fear of other people is limiting your life, that deserves a qualified professional rather than a training drill.
