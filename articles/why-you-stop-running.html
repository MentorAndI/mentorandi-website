The real reason you stopped running

It was not discipline, and it was not the weather. Researchers asked people one simple question during a single session — and the answer predicted how much they were still exercising a year later.

From Health & Fitness Mentor·7 min read

The pattern is so common it is almost a genre. You decide to start running. The first week is hard but satisfying. The second is worse. By week five something comes up, and then something else, and by week eight the trainers are by the door as an accusation.

The story you tell about it afterwards is always the same: you are not disciplined enough. You lack willpower. Other people just want it more.

There is a much better explanation, and it has been measured.

The question that predicted a year

Williams and colleagues took a group of healthy but sedentary adults and had them complete a single bout of moderate-intensity exercise. During it, they asked a very simple thing: how do you feel right now — good or bad, on a simple scale.

Then they came back at six months and twelve months to see how much physical activity those people were actually doing.

The affective response — whether it had felt pleasant — predicted how active they were half a year later, even after accounting for how active they were to begin with and how they felt before starting.

The size of it is what makes it land. A one-point shift on the feeling scale during that single session was associated with around 38 more minutes of physical activity per week, six months later.

Not their intentions. Not their goals. Whether it felt nice at the time — that was the thing that predicted whether they were still doing it.

Which points straight at the mistake

Now put that next to what a beginner actually does on their first run.

They go out and run at the pace that feels like running. For someone unfit, that is well above the intensity their body can comfortably sustain — heart rate climbing, breathing ragged, the whole thing turning into an endurance of unpleasantness with a clock attached.

They finish, relieved rather than pleased. They have just generated exactly the affective response that the research associates with not being there in six months.

And then, because the received wisdom says exercise is supposed to be hard, they do it again the same way. The unpleasantness is interpreted as the point rather than as a signal that the dose is wrong.

This is why so many people conclude they hate running. Most of them have never actually tried running at a pace they could enjoy. They have only ever tried running too fast.

The honest size of the evidence

One caution, because we would rather you had it from us: this was a small study — thirty-seven people. That is not a large sample, and a single finding of this kind should be held with appropriate looseness.

What makes it worth acting on anyway is that the recommendation costs nothing and risks nothing. "Go slower and see whether you enjoy it more" is not a claim that requires extraordinary evidence, and if the effect is smaller than this study suggests, you have still had a more pleasant six weeks.

What to do differently

Run slower than feels right. Considerably slower. The test is conversation: if you could not speak a full sentence comfortably, you are going too fast for the purpose. Most beginners find this humbling, because the sustainable pace can feel embarrassingly close to walking.

Walk on purpose, not as a failure. Alternating running and walking is not a lesser version of running. It is the method that keeps the intensity in the range that people come back to. Treating a walking break as evidence of weakness is how a workable plan gets abandoned.

Judge the session on how you felt, not what the watch said. Given what predicted adherence, "did I enjoy that" is a more useful metric in the first months than pace or distance. Those matter later, once there is something to be consistent about.

Finish wanting slightly more. Stopping while it still feels good is the single most useful habit available to a beginner. Draining yourself completely produces a memory that argues against the next session.

And expect the intensity to earn its place later. None of this says hard training is bad — it is how you get faster, and experienced runners often genuinely enjoy it. But that comes after the habit exists, not before. Hard sessions are something a running habit can afford. They are not how you buy one.

The reframe worth keeping

If you have started running three times and stopped three times, the most likely explanation is not a character flaw. It is that each attempt was run at a pace that made the activity unpleasant, and you responded to sustained unpleasantness the way any sensible person does.

That is a dosing error, and dosing errors are easy to fix. Which is a considerably more useful conclusion than deciding you are the sort of person who does not stick at things.

This is general information about everyday exercise, not medical advice, and an AI mentor does not diagnose or treat anything. Before starting to run — particularly if you have a heart or joint condition, are pregnant, are returning after illness or injury, or have been inactive for a long period — please speak to your GP or a qualified professional. Stop and seek medical advice if you experience chest pain, unusual breathlessness or dizziness.
