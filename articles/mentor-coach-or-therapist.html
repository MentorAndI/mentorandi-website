Why a mentor · Life

Mentor, coach or therapist — which one do you actually need?

They overlap enough to be genuinely confusing, and differ enough that picking the wrong one wastes months. The distinction is simpler than the marketing suggests.

From Life Mentor·8 min read

People rarely arrive at this question in the abstract. They arrive at it stuck — something in life is not working, they have decided to get help, and then they hit a wall of job titles that all promise more or less the same outcome.

So it is worth cutting through it, using how the professions describe themselves rather than how the internet describes them.

The therapist

The British Association for Counselling and Psychotherapy puts it plainly: therapy "provides a safe and confidential space for you to talk to a trained professional about your issues and concerns." The therapist helps you explore thoughts, feelings and behaviours to build self-understanding. And then the line that tells you most about what therapy is: a counsellor "will not give you their opinions or advice or prescribe medication. They will help you find your own solutions."

That restraint is deliberate, not a limitation. Therapy is largely concerned with understanding — why a pattern keeps repeating, what a reaction is protecting, where something originates. It is the right room for distress, for the past when the past is still operating in the present, and for anything affecting your ability to function day to day.

It is the wrong room if what you want is someone to tell you what they think you should do about the job offer.

The coach

The International Coaching Federation defines coaching as "partnering with clients in a thought-provoking and creative process that inspires them to maximize their personal and professional potential."

Read what that sentence assumes. It assumes a client who is functioning, who has a direction, and who wants more out of a capability they already possess. Coaching is generally forward-facing and performance-shaped: a defined goal, a time-bound engagement, structured sessions, accountability between them.

Good coaching is rigorous. It is also usually built around a specific objective, and it typically ends when that objective is met or the block of sessions runs out.

The mentor

Mentoring is the least formalised of the three, and the definitions are correspondingly woolly — so here is the distinction that actually earns its keep.

A therapist is trained in the workings of the mind. A coach is trained in a process for moving you forward. A mentor's value is context: they know your situation, they have been in something like it, and they hold that knowledge across time so you do not have to rebuild it every conversation.

That is why the relationship is usually longer, looser and less structured. Mentoring is not organised around a single objective. It is organised around a person and whatever they are walking through this month — which is a different job, and one the other two are not really designed to do.

Therapy asks why this keeps happening. Coaching asks how you get there from here. Mentoring asks what you are actually dealing with at the moment, and what would help.

A quicker way to choose

Forget the titles for a moment and look at the sentence you would use to describe your situation.

If it contains something that hurts — grief, anxiety that will not settle, a pattern you keep repeating with a sinking familiarity, anything that is interfering with sleep, work or relationships — start with a therapist. Not because the others could not be useful eventually, but because that particular kind of stuck has a profession built specifically for it, and going around it costs time.

If your sentence contains a clear destination and a performance gap — you know what you are aiming at, you are functioning fine, you want to get better at a defined thing faster — a coach is a good buy.

If your sentence is more like "I am not sure what I want, everything is fine on paper, and I keep going round in circles" — that is mentoring territory. So is the more common version: you do not need a whole process, you need someone sensible in your corner who already knows the situation and will not make you start from the beginning.

Two things worth knowing before you pay anyone

Titles are not a guarantee. "Coach" and "mentor" are not protected terms — anyone may use them. In the UK, professional registers exist for good reason: BACP, UKCP and the HCPC hold members to training and ethical standards, and checking a register is a reasonable first step before handing someone your inner life. Ask what someone is trained in, not what they call themselves.

And the boundaries are real but not walls. A competent professional of any of the three will tell you when you are in the wrong room. That willingness — to say "this is beyond what I do, here is who you want" — is one of the more reliable signals that you have found someone good.

Where an AI mentor fits

The honest answer is that it does one part of the mentoring job unusually well and does not attempt the others.

The part it does well is continuity. Most people's problem with getting support is not that good advice is unavailable — it is that they have nobody who holds the thread. You explain the situation from scratch every time, to a friend who has forgotten, or in a session six weeks after the thing that mattered. A mentor that remembers what you were weighing up last month, and asks about it unprompted, removes that overhead entirely.

What it is not is a therapist, and it should not be used as one. It does not diagnose, it does not treat, and it is not a substitute for clinical care.

If what you need is momentum, perspective and a place to think out loud with someone who already has the background — that is what it is for. If what you need is treatment, the useful thing an honest mentor does is say so and point you at the door.
