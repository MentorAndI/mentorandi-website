ADHD isn't a willpower problem — and treating it like one is why nothing works

"Try harder" assumes the difficulty is in wanting it enough. The most influential model in the field describes something else entirely, and it points somewhere far more useful.

From ADHD Mentor·7 min read

Here is the thing that makes the willpower explanation so persuasive: it fits the evidence you have about yourself.

You know exactly what needs doing. You could describe the task, its deadline, the consequences of missing it, and the first step, in detail, to anyone who asked. You are not confused. You are not unaware. And still, for two hours, you did not open the file.

From the outside — and, unfortunately, from the inside — that looks like a straightforward failure of wanting. If you knew and you didn't, what else is left but not caring enough?

Quite a lot, as it turns out.

What the model actually says

The most cited framework in this area is Russell Barkley's, published in Psychological Bulletin in 1997. Its central proposal is that the core difficulty is not attention in the everyday sense, and not motivation in the moral sense. It is behavioural inhibition — the capacity to pause between an impulse and an action, and to hold that pause open long enough for anything else to happen in the gap.

That sounds narrow until you see what Barkley argues depends on it. In his model, the ability to inhibit is what makes room for four other functions to operate: working memory, the self-regulation of feeling and motivation, the internalisation of speech — the running commentary you use to instruct yourself — and the capacity to break a problem apart and rebuild it as a plan.

Read that list again with a stuck afternoon in mind. Holding the goal in mind while doing something else. Generating your own sense of urgency when the deadline is still abstract. Talking yourself through a sequence. Turning "sort out the tax thing" into an actual first move.

Those are not motivational capacities. They are executive ones, and the model describes a difficulty in the machinery that runs them.

The gap is not between knowing and caring. It is between knowing and doing — and that is a different mechanism, which responds to different things.

Why "try harder" is worse than useless

If the difficulty were a shortage of effort, then applying more effort would be the correct treatment. That is the logic behind every version of the advice you have already had: be more disciplined, want it more, stop making excuses.

It fails for a specific reason. Effort is applied at the moment of performance — and the moment of performance is precisely where the difficulty sits. Telling someone to summon more of the thing that is hardest to summon, at the exact moment it is hardest to summon it, is not a strategy. It is a description of the problem, delivered as if it were the solution.

Then there is the second cost, which is quieter and does more damage. Every failed attempt under the willpower model produces evidence — not about the strategy, but about you. Fifteen years of that and most people arrive with a settled belief that they are fundamentally unreliable. That belief does its own work: it makes you less likely to try, quicker to abandon, and much more likely to hide the thing you are behind on rather than ask for help with it.

Shame is not a side effect here. It is an active ingredient in why the next attempt goes badly.

What follows instead

If the difficulty is in executing rather than in wanting, then the useful question stops being how do I make myself care more and becomes how do I need less from myself at the moment it counts.

That reframe is not a consolation prize. It changes what you build.

It means putting the plan somewhere outside your head, where it does not depend on remembering — the logic of externalising structure, which is the most reliably useful idea in this whole area.

It means making the entry point to a task so small that starting requires almost nothing, because the start is where it breaks, not the middle.

It means attaching what you want to do to something already happening, so a cue in the world does the work your memory was failing to do — the same mechanism behind habit stacking.

And it means treating a missed day as a design problem rather than a character verdict. Not because that is kinder, though it is, but because "the trigger was too weak" leads to a fix and "I am useless" leads to another week of not opening the file.

The part that takes longest

Understanding this intellectually takes about ten minutes. Believing it takes considerably longer, because the willpower story has usually had decades of practice and a lot of corroborating witnesses.

What tends to shift it is not an argument. It is evidence — a run of instances where you changed the design rather than the effort and the thing actually got done. That is why a single insight rarely holds on its own, and why having something that remembers what you tried, what worked, and what state you were in when it didn't, tends to matter more than any individual technique.

This piece is about everyday executive function — starting, planning, following through. It is not diagnostic and it is not clinical advice. An AI mentor does not diagnose ADHD, recommend or comment on medication, or replace assessment and care from a qualified professional. If you think ADHD may be affecting your life, that is a conversation for a doctor or a specialist clinician.
