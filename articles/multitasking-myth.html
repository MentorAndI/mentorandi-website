You're not multitasking. You're paying a toll every time you switch.

The research on this is solid and old. The statistic everyone quotes about it is neither — and the exception is more interesting than the rule.

From Focus Mentor·7 min read

Multitasking is one of the few myths that people defend personally. Tell someone that habits take longer than three weeks and they shrug. Tell them they cannot write an email while listening to a meeting and they will explain, with some warmth, that they specifically can.

So let us be precise about what the evidence says, because the popular version is both overstated and, in one important respect, wrong.

What actually happens

For anything demanding enough to require attention, you are not running two processes side by side. You are alternating — and the alternation is not free.

The clearest demonstration comes from Rubinstein, Meyer and Evans, who ran four experiments in which young adults switched back and forth between tasks such as solving arithmetic problems and classifying geometric shapes. Every switch cost time. That much was expected. The useful findings were about what changed the size of the toll.

Switch costs grew with the complexity of the rules involved. Alternating between two trivial tasks was cheap; alternating between two that each required holding a set of rules in mind was expensive. And switch costs shrank when the task was cued — when something told you what was coming, so you were not also paying to work out which mode you were meant to be in.

That gives you the shape of the problem. The cost is not really "doing two things". It is reloading — swapping out the rules, goals and context that the previous task had loaded, and swapping in another set.

The expensive part is not the work. It is the reloading — and you pay it every single time, whether or not you notice.

The statistic you should stop repeating

Somewhere in the retelling of this research, a figure attached itself: that multitasking costs you around 40% of your productive time. You will see it in slide decks, in articles, and occasionally attributed directly to the study above.

Treat it with suspicion. The experiments measured switching costs in laboratory tasks, in fractions of a second per switch, under controlled conditions. Turning that into a clean percentage of a working day requires assumptions the research does not supply — how often you switch, between what, at what complexity. A figure that specific, applied that generally, is doing more work than the data behind it can support.

This matters beyond pedantry. When the argument for single-tasking rests on a number that does not hold up, the whole case looks like productivity folklore — and the underlying finding, which is robust, gets discarded with it.

The honest version is less quotable and more useful: switching between demanding tasks reliably costs time, the cost rises with complexity, and it falls when transitions are cued rather than improvised. You do not need a percentage to act on that.

The 2.5%

Now the genuinely interesting part, and the reason the person arguing with you is not simply wrong.

Watson and Strayer put two hundred people in a high-fidelity driving simulator and had them perform a demanding memory-and-arithmetic task at the same time. Almost everyone got worse — at the driving, at the task, or both.

Except that 2.5% of the sample showed no measurable decrement at all. They performed as well doing both as doing either alone, and scored in the top quarter on every measure. The researchers called them supertaskers.

So a small number of people really are exceptional at this. Two things follow, and the second is the one that matters.

The first: the rule is not universal, and anyone claiming multitasking is flatly impossible is overreaching.

The second: the base rate is one in forty, and confidence is a poor guide to membership. The people most certain they can multitask are drawn from the whole population, not from that 2.5%. Unless you have specifically been measured, the responsible assumption is that you are in the ninety-seven and a half percent — which is exactly where everyone reading this feels they are the exception.

What to actually do about it

The advice "stop multitasking" is not useful, because most switching is not chosen. It is imposed by notifications, colleagues and a calendar built by someone else. So aim at the switching rate rather than at your willpower.

Batch by mode, not by priority. The costly switch is between kinds of thinking — writing, then a spreadsheet, then a difficult conversation. Grouping tasks that use the same mental setup means fewer reloads, even when the tasks themselves are unrelated.

Cue your transitions. The research found cuing reduced the cost. In practice that means deciding what comes next before you finish the current thing, rather than surfacing from a task into an open question about what to do now. That open question is where twenty minutes goes.

Write down where you stopped. A single line naming your place removes most of the reload cost when you return. It is the cheapest intervention here and the one people skip — and it is the same move as externalising structure.

Reduce what can interrupt you, rather than resisting interruption. Resisting is a per-event cost you pay all day. Closing the channel is paid once.

The bit nobody mentions

Most people who fix this do it for a fortnight and then drift back, because the switching was never really a technique problem. It was an environment that rewarded looking responsive, and a set of habits that formed around it over years.

Which is the ordinary shape of most of these things: the insight is cheap, the change is not, and what determines the outcome is whether anyone notices when it lapses.

Sources

  1. Rubinstein, J. S., Meyer, D. E., & Evans, J. E. (2001). Executive control of cognitive processes in task switching. Journal of Experimental Psychology: Human Perception and Performance, 27(4), 763–797. Four experiments; switching costs increased with rule complexity and decreased with task cuing.
  2. Watson, J. M., & Strayer, D. L. (2010). Supertaskers: Profiles in extraordinary multitasking ability. Psychonomic Bulletin & Review, 17(4), 479–485. Of 200 participants tested in a driving simulator with a concurrent OSPAN task, 2.5% showed no performance decrement under dual-task conditions.
