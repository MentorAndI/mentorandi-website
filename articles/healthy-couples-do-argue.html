Healthy couples do argue — what matters is how it starts

The belief that good relationships are conflict-free does more damage than most arguments do. And the research points at something oddly specific: the opening.

From Relationship Mentor·8 min read

Somewhere along the way, a lot of people absorbed the idea that arguing is a symptom. That couples who are right for each other simply do not have these rows, and that every fight is a small piece of evidence in a case slowly being built about whether the relationship is viable.

It is a strangely durable belief, and it survives despite almost everyone privately knowing that they argue and mostly being fine.

The cost is not the belief itself. It is what people do with it: they either avoid the conversation entirely to keep the record clean, or they have it while quietly terrified, which is not a state in which anyone communicates well.

What the observational work found

John Gottman and Robert Levenson spent decades bringing couples into a laboratory and recording how they discussed disagreements — the words, the tone, the physiology.

Conflict itself did not distinguish the couples who stayed together from those who did not. Everybody argued. What differed was the character of the arguing, and in particular the balance of warmth and hostility inside it.

The specific behaviours they identified as corrosive are worth knowing, because they are recognisable: criticism aimed at the person rather than the problem, defensiveness that refuses any part of the complaint, withdrawal that leaves the other person talking to a wall — and contempt, the sneer, which their work consistently treated as the most damaging of the set.

None of those is "having an argument". They are things that can happen inside one, and they are all avoidable in a way that disagreement is not.

The question was never whether you fight. It is what you are willing to do to each other while fighting.

The first three minutes

Then there is a finding that changed how a lot of people think about this, and it is unusually concrete.

Carrère and Gottman recorded 124 newlywed couples — married less than six months — discussing a conflict, and coded the emotional content of the conversation in five three-minute blocks. They then followed the couples for six years.

Using only the first three minutes, they could distinguish the couples who would later divorce from those who would not. Of the seventeen couples who divorced, all had opened their conflict discussions with markedly more negative and less positive emotion than the couples who stayed together.

The opening, in other words, contained most of the information. Not the topic, not the length, not who was right. How it began.

There is a second detail in that study which is easy to miss and quietly important. How harshly a conversation started was related to what had happened before it — specifically to whether the partners had been interested and warm with each other during an ordinary chat about the events of the day. The quality of the everyday friendship shaped how the difficult conversation opened.

An honest word about the famous numbers

You will have seen the claim that this research predicts divorce with somewhere around ninety per cent accuracy. It is worth being careful with it, and we would rather say so than repeat it.

Heyman and Smith Slep published a direct methodological criticism of exactly this: that these figures generally come from fitting a statistical model to a dataset after the outcomes are already known, rather than from applying that model to a fresh set of couples and seeing whether it works. Fitting a model to data you already have will always look impressive. The test that matters — cross-validation on a new sample — is a different and harder thing, and with small samples, patterns that look important are often flukes.

So: treat the descriptive findings as the solid part. Couples argue. Contempt is corrosive. Conversations that open badly tend to go badly. Treat the headline accuracy percentages as marketing rather than science.

That distinction is worth making because the overclaiming has caused people to dismiss the whole body of work, which is also a mistake. The observations are useful. The predictions were oversold.

What to actually do with this

Stop counting arguments. The number is not the metric, and treating it as one adds a layer of dread to every disagreement, which reliably makes the disagreement worse.

Watch the opening sentence. If the finding about the first three minutes carries any lesson, it is that an enormous amount rides on how you begin. "You never think about anyone but yourself" and "I've been feeling a bit invisible this week" concern the same grievance and start two entirely different conversations. The second one is not weaker. It is simply survivable.

Notice contempt specifically. Not irritation, not raised voices — contempt: the eye-roll, the mimicking tone, the joke that is not one. Of all the behaviours in this literature, that is the one worth treating as a genuine alarm rather than a bad mood.

Take the everyday chat seriously. The friendship maintained on ordinary evenings appears to shape how the hard conversations open. That is a slightly deflating conclusion — the work is not done during the argument, it is done in the fortnight beforehand — but it is also a hopeful one, because ordinary attention is easier to give than heroic communication under pressure.

Prepare the difficult ones. If the opening matters this much, the opening is worth thinking about before you are in it. That is a whole technique in itself, and it is worth doing properly.

The reassuring part

If you have been treating your arguments as evidence, you can stop. The presence of conflict tells you almost nothing about the health of a relationship — it mostly tells you that two people with separate inner lives are attempting to run one shared life.

What the evidence does suggest is that a smaller thing than you think is doing the damage, and that it happens in the first minute of the conversation rather than across the twenty that follow. That is a much more tractable problem than "we argue".
