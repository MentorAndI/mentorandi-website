Worry time: give it an appointment instead of the whole day

The idea sounds too simple to work, and the evidence is genuinely there — though smaller than most articles about it will tell you.

From Stress & Burnout Mentor·7 min read

Worry has an unusual property: it is almost never confined. It does not arrive, get considered, and leave. It arrives at 7am in the shower, again in the meeting, again at the traffic lights, again at 11pm — the same three sentences, in the same order, producing the same nothing.

You have probably tried to stop it, which does not work, and possibly been told not to think about it, which works even less well. Worry time takes a different approach: it does not ask you to worry less. It asks you to worry somewhere specific.

The technique

You choose a fixed slot — commonly about thirty minutes, at the same time each day, in the same place. Not late in the evening, for obvious reasons.

When a worry turns up outside that slot, you do three things. You notice it. You write down one line so it is recorded rather than held. And you postpone it, explicitly, to the appointment.

Then, during the slot, you worry. Properly, deliberately, on purpose, working through what you wrote down — and where something can be problem-solved, you problem-solve it.

That last part is not decoration. The point is not to suppress the worry; it is to relocate it. If the appointment never happens, you have simply spent the day telling yourself "not now" to something that then has to shout louder.

You are not trying to have fewer worries. You are trying to stop having the same one eleven times.

Where it comes from, and what the evidence says

The technique originates with Borkovec and colleagues in the early 1980s, as a piece of clinical work on excessive worry. It has since been studied in its own right.

In one controlled study, fifty-three people who worried a great deal were randomly assigned either to two weeks of this method — a thirty-minute worry period, restricted by time and place — or to a control condition. Afterwards, the worry-period group did better on measures of worry, anxiety, negative affect and, notably, insomnia.

Now the honest part, which most write-ups omit.

A 2023 meta-analysis pooled the studies on worry postponement and found that it does reduce both the frequency and the duration of everyday worry. But when it was compared against a fairer comparison — people who simply recorded their worries without postponing them — the effects were small: around 0.31 for how long worry lasted and 0.19 for how often it happened.

That matters for how you should hold this. Some of the benefit comes from postponing. A meaningful chunk seems to come from the much duller act of writing the thing down at all. Which is not a reason to skip the technique — it is a reason to treat the notebook as the non-negotiable part, and to be suspicious of anyone promising transformation.

How to run it without it collapsing

Same time, same place, every day. The place matters more than people expect — that is what "stimulus control" means. If you worry at the kitchen table for twenty minutes daily, the kitchen table becomes where worry belongs, and the bed becomes somewhere it is less at home.

Not before bed. The most common self-sabotage is scheduling it at 10pm, which hands your worst thinking the hours with the least perspective. Late afternoon or early evening works better for most people.

Write the line at the moment of postponing. Ten words is plenty. The writing is doing real work — it converts a loop into an entry, so the loop has somewhere to stop.

Actually attend the appointment. Skip it repeatedly and the postponement stops being credible to you. If nothing is left worth worrying about when the slot arrives — which happens often — spend two minutes confirming that and finish early. That counts.

Separate the two kinds. Some entries are problems, which have a next action. Others are simply uncertainties, which do not. Sorting them is most of the value of the half hour; treating an uncertainty as a problem to be solved is what generates the loop in the first place.

Give it two weeks. That is the duration used in the research, and it is roughly how long it takes before postponing stops feeling absurd.

Where it does not apply

Two limits worth stating plainly.

It is a technique for recurring, circular worry — the kind that repeats without progressing. It is not for genuine problem-solving under a deadline, where the right response is to think it through now and act.

And it comes from a clinical tradition, which is worth being straightforward about. Used as an everyday self-management habit it is perfectly sensible. It is not treatment, and if worry is dominating your days, interfering with sleep over a long period, or accompanied by panic or persistent low mood, the useful step is a professional rather than a better technique. Reaching for a self-help method when the situation has outgrown one is a common way to lose six months.

This describes an everyday self-management technique, presented for general wellbeing in ordinary working life. It is not diagnostic and not clinical advice, and it is not a treatment for anxiety or any other condition. An AI mentor does not diagnose or treat, and does not replace a qualified professional. If worry, low mood or sleeplessness is persistent or affecting your ability to function, please speak to a doctor or a qualified therapist.
