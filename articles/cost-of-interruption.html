Interruptions don't slow you down. They charge you somewhere else.

Researchers interrupted people mid-task and measured what happened. The work got finished faster. The bill turned up in a different currency entirely.

From Focus Mentor·7 min read

Everyone knows the number. Get interrupted and it takes something like twenty-three minutes to get back to what you were doing. It appears in productivity books, in articles about open-plan offices, in the slide your manager showed at the away day.

The number has a real research lineage, but it has been detached from its context so thoroughly that it now functions as folklore. And the more interesting result — from the same body of work — is one almost nobody repeats, probably because it sounds like it argues the wrong way.

What they actually found

Gloria Mark, Daniela Gudith and Ulrich Klocke ran an experiment in which people were interrupted while working, and measured what the interruption cost them.

The headline result: people completed the interrupted tasks in less time, with no difference in quality.

Read that again, because it is the opposite of what the folklore claims. Interrupted work was not slower or worse. If your only measure is output, the interruption did not show up at all.

The cost was real, but it was charged elsewhere. People compensated for being interrupted by working faster — and paid for that compensation in stress, frustration, time pressure and effort. The task got done. Something in the person did not survive it intact.

The work absorbs the interruption. You absorb the cost of the work absorbing it.

Why this is worse news, not better

The instinctive reading is reassuring: interruptions do not really hurt, so stop worrying.

That is exactly backwards, for a structural reason.

If interruptions made work visibly slower, organisations would fix them. Missed deadlines are legible; they show up in project plans and get escalated. A cost that appears as elevated stress and effort, day after day, is invisible to every system designed to notice problems — while being entirely visible to the person paying it.

Which means the compensation itself is the trap. You are quietly subsidising a badly designed environment out of your own reserves, and because the output looks fine, nothing prompts anyone to change it. The feedback loop that would normally correct the situation has been severed by your own competence.

That has an obvious endpoint, and it is not a productivity problem. Sustained over months, "kept up with everything while running on elevated stress and effort" is the ordinary description of how people arrive at burnout — via a period in which, by every available metric, they were performing well.

What follows from it

Stop measuring your day by whether it got done. It got done. That was never the question, and it is a poor signal precisely because it stays green while the underlying cost accumulates. The better question is what a normal working day is costing you to produce.

Treat the interruption rate as an environmental fact you can change. Resisting interruptions is a per-event tax you pay all day. Reducing what is able to interrupt you is paid once. Closing a channel beats defending against it.

Protect the work that cannot absorb the hit. Some work genuinely does survive interruption — mechanical tasks with an obvious next step. Other work does not: anything requiring you to hold a thread. Put that in a defended window, which is the whole argument for energy-window scheduling.

Make re-entry cheap. When you are interrupted, note where you stopped before you turn away — one line. Part of what you are paying for on return is reconstruction, and reconstruction is the part you can eliminate outright. It also reduces the residue the unfinished task leaves running in the background.

Notice the compensating. The tell is the feeling of working faster than you would choose to. That sensation is not diligence. It is the mechanism in the study, running in you, in real time — and it is the earliest signal available that a day is costing more than it should.

The honest scope

This was a controlled experiment with a specific design, not a description of every workplace. It does not establish that interruptions are harmless, nor that they cause burnout — no single study could.

What it does establish is more precise and more useful: the cost of an interrupted day is not primarily measured in minutes, so measuring minutes will tell you that everything is fine. If you want to know what your working conditions are actually costing, output is the wrong instrument. How you feel at the end of the day is the better one — which is an unfashionable thing to say about productivity, and, on this evidence, the accurate one.

Sources

  1. Mark, G., Gudith, D., & Klocke, U. (2008). The cost of interrupted work: More speed and stress. Proceedings of the SIGCHI Conference on Human Factors in Computing Systems (CHI '08). Interrupted tasks were completed in less time with no difference in quality; participants compensated by working faster and reported higher stress, frustration, time pressure and effort. The study also found individual differences, with openness to experience and need for personal structure predicting disruption costs.
  2. Leroy, S. (2009). Why is it so hard to do my work? The challenge of attention residue when switching between work tasks. Organizational Behavior and Human Decision Processes, 109(2), 168–181.
