Only 7% of communication is words? That's not what the study said.

It is the most quoted statistic in communication training, it comes from two small experiments about single ambiguous words, and its author has spent decades asking people to stop.

From Charisma Mentor·7 min read

You have seen the pie chart. Seven per cent of communication is the words you use, thirty-eight per cent is your tone of voice, and fifty-five per cent is your body language. It appears in presentation courses, sales training, dating advice and roughly every LinkedIn post about executive presence.

Before we take it apart, notice what it implies. If ninety-three per cent of meaning were carried by tone and body, you could follow a lecture in a language you have never heard and get most of it. You could read a physics paper aloud in a warm voice and communicate physics. Obviously you cannot.

So where did the number come from?

Two experiments, 1967

Albert Mehrabian, then at UCLA, published two studies that year, and the famous ratio is a combination of them.

In the first, participants heard a single word spoken in different tones — positive, neutral or negative — and had to judge the speaker's feeling. The question was narrow: when the word and the tone point in different directions, which one do listeners follow?

The second study added a face. Participants judged attitude from a combination of vocal tone and facial expression.

Put the two together and you can derive the weights: about seven per cent word, thirty-eight per cent tone, fifty-five per cent face. Which is exactly what someone did, and it escaped into the world without its conditions attached.

The finding was about how people guess at a feeling when the signals conflict. It was never about how much of communication is words.

The conditions that got lost

Three of them, and each is enough to sink the general version.

The studies concerned attitudes and feelings — whether someone liked you, not whether they understood you. Nothing in them addresses information, instruction, explanation or argument, which is most of what people actually say to each other.

The stimuli were single words, often deliberately ambiguous ones like "maybe" or "dear". A word with almost no content leaves very little for the verbal channel to carry, so of course the listener leans on tone and face. That is a feature of the design, not a discovery about conversation.

And the interesting cases were the ones where the channels contradicted each other — a warm word in a cold voice. The ratio describes what happens in a conflict, not in ordinary agreement.

Mehrabian himself has been clear about this for years, effectively asking people to stop applying the equations unless the speaker is communicating about their feelings or attitudes. It is unusual for a researcher to have to disown a number this often, and rarer still for it to make no difference.

What is actually true underneath

Here is why the myth has survived: there is something real inside it, and it is worth training.

When your words and your delivery disagree, people believe the delivery. Say "no, that's a great idea" in a flat voice while looking at your laptop, and nobody hears the words. That is genuinely what the research supports, and it matters — because incongruence is common and mostly unintentional.

What does not follow is the thing the myth is used to sell: that content barely matters, so you should spend your preparation on gestures and posture. If you have nothing to say, an open stance will not rescue you. The person who prepared their argument and delivered it plainly will beat the person who practised their hand movements, every time.

What to train instead

Congruence, not performance. The useful question is not "what should my hands do" but "does my delivery match what I actually mean?" Most people's problem is not a lack of gestures; it is that they say something is fine while radiating that it is not.

The content first. Know the one sentence you want the other person to leave with. Delivery amplifies a clear point and cannot manufacture one.

Then the channel that carries feeling. Where the research does apply — signalling warmth, interest, disagreement — tone and face are doing most of the work, so that is where to put attention. Being interested is largely conveyed by whether you look interested.

And ask, rather than infer. Since these channels are how people guess at attitudes, and guesses are frequently wrong, the cheapest fix in most conversations is a question. That is a technique in its own right, and the evidence behind it is much stronger than this ratio ever was.

Why this one is worth knowing

Not for the pedantry. Because a great deal of communication training is built on top of it, and if you have been told that words are seven per cent of the job, you have been pointed away from the part you can most reliably improve.

Charisma is trainable. It is just not trainable by treating what you say as a rounding error.
