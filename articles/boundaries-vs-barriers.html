Boundaries vs barriers: the difference decides whether it holds

Almost all boundary advice assumes more separation is better. The research points somewhere more interesting — and more forgiving.

From Stress & Burnout Mentor·7 min read

Most people have tried the standard advice. No email after six. Phone in the other room. A hard line between work and everything else, defended absolutely.

And most people have watched it collapse, usually within a fortnight, usually at the worst moment — and then concluded that they are bad at boundaries.

There is a distinction worth making here, because it explains both the collapse and what to do instead.

Two different things wearing one word

A barrier is absolute. It does not bend, it has no exceptions, and its integrity depends on never being crossed. No work after six, full stop. Because it cannot bend, the first genuine exception — a real crisis, a colleague in trouble, a deadline that actually matters — does not bend it either. It breaks it. And once broken, it is gone, because a rule with one exception is no longer a rule you believe in.

A boundary is a rule with a stated shape, including what happens at the edges. Not "no email after six" but "I don't answer email after six unless it's from the on-call channel, and if I do answer, I stop again afterwards." It survives contact with reality because reality was accounted for in the design.

Barriers feel stronger. They are the ones people announce. But they are brittle, and brittleness in a rule you have announced is expensive — you do not just lose the rule, you lose some confidence in your ability to hold one.

A barrier breaks the first time life is legitimately complicated. A boundary was built expecting that.

What the research actually found

Here is where the usual advice goes wrong, and it is not a small error.

Almost everything written about work-life balance assumes a direction: more separation is better, and the goal is to maximise it. Glen Kreiner tested that assumption with 325 employees across a wide range of jobs and organisations, and what he found was not about the amount of separation at all.

It was about fit: the match between how much separation a person wants and how much their workplace actually supplies. Where those two lined up, people reported less work-home conflict, less stress and more job satisfaction. Where they did not, the mismatch itself did the damage — and the effects were asymmetric, meaning that being over-separated and under-separated are not simply mirror images of each other.

The paper explicitly challenges the earlier line that integrating work and home is the way to reduce conflict and stress.

Read what follows from that. There is no universally correct boundary. The colleague who happily answers messages at nine in the evening is not less disciplined than you, and the one who goes dark at five is not more virtuous. They have different preferences, and what determines their stress is whether their job lets them have the arrangement they want.

Which means the first question is not "where's my line"

It is: what do I actually want, and what is this job actually giving me?

Those are two separate enquiries and people usually skip both, going straight to a rule copied from someone whose preferences they have never checked against their own.

Start with your preference, honestly. Some people genuinely feel better with work and home interleaved — a message answered on the sofa costs them nothing and buys them flexibility. Others need a clean edge and pay disproportionately for every intrusion. Neither is the mature option. Aspiring to the wrong one for yourself is how people end up enforcing rules that make them miserable in a new way.

Then look at what your work supplies. Not what its values page says — what actually happens. Are messages sent at ten at night? Are they expected to be read? Is there a genuine on-call rota, or has everyone silently agreed to be permanently available?

The gap between those two answers is your problem, stated precisely. And a precise problem has options a vague one does not.

Building one that survives

Name the exception before you need it. This is the whole difference between the two things. Decide now what genuinely warrants breaking the pattern — and make the list short and specific. An unnamed exception becomes a precedent; a named one stays an exception.

Say what happens after the exception. The damage is rarely the one evening you worked. It is that the boundary never came back. "I'll take this one, and I'm off again tomorrow evening" is the sentence that preserves it.

Make it about behaviour, not availability. "I'm not available" invites negotiation. "I check messages at nine and at four" is a description of how you work, which is much harder to argue with and much easier to keep.

Aim at the mismatch, not at yourself. If the gap between your preference and your job's supply is large and permanent, no personal rule closes it. That is a conversation about the role — or about the job — and it is better had deliberately than discovered eighteen months later. This is also the mechanism behind why a holiday doesn't fix burnout: the situation is the variable, and a fortnight away does not change it.

Expect to renegotiate. Preferences shift with small children, illness, a new manager, a good year. A boundary set once and defended forever is a barrier again by another route.

The part that is genuinely hard

None of this addresses the case where your preference is reasonable, the mismatch is real, and you have very little power to change either. That happens, and pretending a better-phrased rule solves it would be dishonest.

What the fit finding does give you, even then, is a more accurate account of what is going on. You are not failing at boundaries. You are in a job whose supply does not match your preference — which is a statement about a situation rather than about your character, and situations are the kind of thing that can, eventually, be changed.

This piece is about everyday working life and how people arrange the edges of it. It is not diagnostic and not clinical advice. The rule isn't the hard part. Holding it in week six is.

Every boundary survives the first fortnight. What breaks it is one legitimate exception, followed by a quiet failure to return — and nobody is watching closely enough to point that out. That is precisely what a mentor is for. It remembers what you decided, notices the drift, and helps you rebuild the one clause that stopped working instead of scrapping the whole thing.
