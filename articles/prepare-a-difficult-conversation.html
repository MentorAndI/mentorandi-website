How to prepare a difficult conversation

Most people prepare by rehearsing their argument, which is preparation for a court case. The evidence suggests something narrower and far more useful.

From Relationship Mentor·8 min read

There is a version of preparing that almost everyone does and almost nobody benefits from. You lie awake assembling the case. The evidence, the examples with dates, the anticipated objections and your answers to them. By morning you have a closing argument.

Then you deliver the first line of it, and within ninety seconds you are in a different conversation entirely — one about tone, and about who is being unfair, and about something from March.

The preparation was not wasted because you prepared. It was wasted because you prepared the wrong part.

Why the opening carries the weight

Carrère and Gottman recorded 124 newlywed couples discussing a conflict and coded the emotional content in three-minute blocks, then followed them for six years. Using only the first three minutes, they could distinguish the couples who would later divorce from those who would not — all seventeen of the couples who divorced had begun their discussions with markedly more negativity and less warmth.

Whatever you make of the prediction claims attached to this body of work — and there are good reasons to be careful with them — the descriptive finding is the useful bit: the opening carries a disproportionate amount of what happens next.

That is unusually good news, because the opening is the one part of a difficult conversation you can actually control. You cannot script their responses. You cannot control the middle. You can decide, in advance, the first two sentences out of your mouth.

You are not preparing an argument. You are preparing about forty words.

The preparation that works

Work out what you actually want

Before anything else, answer this honestly: what would a good outcome look like? Not "for them to understand" — that is a feeling, not an outcome. Something concrete: a change in how something gets handled, a decision made, an apology, or simply for them to know a thing they currently do not.

A surprising number of difficult conversations go badly because the person raising it does not know what they want, so it becomes a general presentation of grievance, and general grievance is almost impossible to respond to well.

Sometimes the honest answer is "I want them to feel bad about this." That is a human thing to want, and worth noticing privately, because a conversation aimed at that will get it and nothing else.

Write the first two sentences

Literally write them. This is the core of the technique and it takes four minutes.

Three things to check in what you have written. Does it describe the situation rather than their character — "the last three weekends have been booked without asking me" rather than "you're completely inconsiderate"? Does it say what you feel without making the feeling their verdict? And does it end somewhere they can respond to, rather than at the end of a summing-up?

Compare: "You never think about my time." Against: "The last few weekends got planned without me and I've been feeling a bit sidelined. Can we talk about how we do that?"

Same grievance. The second one does not require the other person to accept a character judgement before the conversation can begin.

Decide the one thing

Difficult conversations expand. You raise the weekends and arrive, twenty minutes later, at the wedding in 2019.

Pick one thing. Write it down. When the conversation broadens — and it will, because the other person will bring their own list — you can acknowledge the new item without pursuing it. "That's fair and I want to talk about it properly, but can we finish this one first?"

Prepare their side, not your rebuttal

This is the step people skip, and it is the highest-value one.

Spend five minutes writing the most reasonable version of their position — not the weak version you can dismantle, but the account they would actually give. Do it properly and one of two things happens. Either you find your case is thinner than you thought, which is worth knowing before rather than during. Or you find it holds up, and you go in less defensive, because you are no longer braced for an objection you have not considered.

Choose the moment deliberately

Not late at night. Not in the ten minutes before one of you leaves. Not by message, where tone is invented by the reader.

And ideally not cold. The research found that how harshly these conversations opened was related to what had come before — whether there had been ordinary warmth and interest in the preceding days. A difficult conversation launched into a fortnight of low-grade distance starts from further back.

During: two things worth holding

The first minute is the whole job. Deliver your two sentences as prepared, then stop talking. The urge to keep going — to add evidence, to pre-empt the response — is strong and it converts an opening into a case.

If it escalates, name it rather than winning it. "This is going somewhere neither of us wants — can we pause and come back after dinner?" is not a retreat. It is a repair attempt, and it is more useful than the point you were about to make.

What preparation cannot do

It cannot make a person hear something they are determined not to hear, and it cannot make a fair request land well with someone who has no intention of moving. If you have run this properly three times and the answer is always a deflection, the problem is no longer your opening.

What it does do is remove a specific, common and entirely avoidable failure — the one where a legitimate grievance gets buried under the way it was raised, and the following two hours are spent on the delivery rather than the substance.

This is general guidance for everyday conversations. It is not therapy, not clinical advice and not a substitute for couples counselling or mediation. Somewhere to draft it before you say it.

Writing the first two sentences works — but only if there is somewhere to write them, and someone to tell you honestly that what you have drafted still reads as an accusation. That is a real use for a mentor. It knows the history, it will not take a side to be nice, and it is available at eleven at night when the conversation is tomorrow.
