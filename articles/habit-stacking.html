Habit stacking: attach the new thing to something you already do

The name is recent. The mechanism underneath it has been tested on more than eight thousand people, and it is one of the few things in this field that reliably works.

From Life Mentor·7 min read

Most attempts at a new routine fail at a very specific moment, and it is not the moment you would guess. It is not the effort. It is the instant just before the effort, when you have to remember the intention, decide it applies now, and decide you are doing it.

That instant is where things die. Not dramatically — you don't refuse. You just never quite arrive at the decision, because nothing in your day asked you to.

Habit stacking removes that instant. Instead of relying on yourself to remember and choose, you tie the new behaviour to something that already happens without fail, and let the old thing do the remembering.

The shape of it

A stack is one sentence, decided in advance:

After I [thing I already do reliably], I will [new thing].

After I put the kettle on, I will empty the dishwasher. After I sit down at my desk, I will write the first three lines. After I brush my teeth, I will lay out tomorrow's clothes.

It looks almost too plain to bother writing down. That plainness is the point, and it is worth understanding why, because the version people usually try — "I'll do more of X this month" — has roughly none of the same properties.

Where the evidence actually comes from

"Habit stacking" is a recent piece of vocabulary, popularised by productivity writers over the last decade or so. If you go looking for studies with that phrase in the title you will be disappointed, and it is worth being straight about that.

What has been studied, extensively, is the underlying structure: the if-then plan. In the research literature it is called an implementation intention, a term introduced by the psychologist Peter Gollwitzer in the late 1990s. The idea is that goals ("get fitter", "read more") specify an outcome but leave the execution unspecified, whereas an implementation intention pins down the moment: when situation X arises, I will perform response Y.

In 2006, Gollwitzer and Paschal Sheeran pooled the evidence — ninety-four independent tests, more than eight thousand participants — and found a medium-to-large effect on goal attainment, d = 0.65. In a field where a great many appealing ideas have shrunk or vanished under scrutiny, that is an unusually durable result, and it has held up across domains from exercise to diet to studying to attending appointments.

So the branding is new. The machinery is not, and the machinery is the part that matters.

Why tying it to a cue works

Two things happen when you specify the moment.

The first is that you stop having to notice the opportunity. An unattached intention requires you to spot, somewhere in a busy day, that now would be a good time. A stacked one is triggered by something you are already doing — the kettle, the desk, the toothbrush — so the cue does the noticing for you.

The second is that the decision has already been made. In the moment, you are not weighing whether to do the thing; you are following a plan you made when you were calm and had perspective, rather than negotiating with the version of you who is tired and would rather not. This is the same reason waiting to feel motivated tends to fail: it puts the decision at the worst possible moment, every single time.

You are not trying to become more disciplined. You are trying to reduce the number of moments where discipline is required.

How to build one that holds

The failures are predictable, which is useful, because it means you can design around them.

Pick an anchor that genuinely never moves. This is where most stacks break. "After breakfast" sounds fixed and isn't — you skip it, you eat at your desk, weekends are different. Ask yourself whether the anchor happened every day last week, including the bad days. If it didn't, it is not an anchor, it is a hope.

Make the cue a specific event, not a period. "In the morning" is not a cue; it is a window, and windows close without anyone noticing. "When my feet hit the floor" is a cue. The more precisely you can name the instant, the less work your memory has to do.

Keep the new action smaller than feels serious. The size of the action determines whether it survives a difficult day, and difficult days are the ones that decide whether a routine sticks. Two minutes of the thing, done on a bad Tuesday, beats twenty minutes done only in a good week.

Build one at a time. The temptation is to design a whole morning — six stacked behaviours, a chain running from alarm to front door. Chains are brittle; break a link and the rest goes with it. One stack, until it is boring, then the next.

Decide the recovery in advance. You will miss. Missing a single day does not undo the progress — but not knowing what to do afterwards often does. Write down what happens the day after a miss, before you need it.

The unglamorous truth

People come to this expecting a lever — something that makes the difficult thing stop being difficult. That is not what this is. Stacking does not make the dishwasher interesting or the writing easy.

What it does is remove one specific failure point: the moment of remembering and deciding. That sounds modest. It is modest. It also happens to be the point where most attempts quietly end, which is why fixing it does more than the more exciting interventions do.
