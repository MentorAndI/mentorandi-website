Opposites don't attract. The data is fairly blunt about it.

One of the most repeated claims about love, tested across millions of couples, and comprehensively not supported. What follows from that is more interesting than the debunking.

From Relationship Mentor·7 min read

It is one of those beliefs that feels like folk wisdom because it has been repeated for a century. The introvert and the extrovert. The planner and the free spirit. Two halves completing each other, the differences supplying what each one lacks.

It makes a good film. As a description of who actually ends up together, it is wrong, and the evidence against it is not marginal.

What the study did

A 2023 paper in Nature Human Behaviour by Horwitz and colleagues did two things at once.

First, a meta-analysis: 199 previous studies, covering millions of couples, examining 22 traits. Second, a fresh analysis of nearly 80,000 couples in the UK Biobank across 133 traits — personality, political leanings, education, drinking habits, and a long list of other things.

The result was consistent in both. For somewhere between 82 and 89 per cent of the traits examined, partners were more likely to be similar than different. Traits where opposites genuinely tended to pair up were rare.

The strongest similarities were not the romantic ones. Political and religious attitudes, level of education, and certain measures of intelligence showed the highest correlations. Personality traits — the very things the myth is usually about — showed real but weaker similarity.

People do not pair up to complete themselves. Mostly, they pair up with someone who already agrees with them about how life works.

Two things this does not mean

The authors themselves cautioned that the correlations they found were fairly modest and should not be overstated — which is a caution worth taking seriously in both directions.

It does not mean difference is a problem. "Similar on most measured traits" is entirely compatible with two people who argue about money, holidays, and how to load a dishwasher. The similarity showing up in the data is not the absence of friction; it is a broad overlap in values and background against which the friction happens.

And it does not mean you should screen partners for likeness. A modest statistical tendency across millions of people tells you almost nothing about whether one particular relationship will work. Using population-level correlations as personal selection criteria is how you end up rejecting someone for a trait that would never have mattered.

Where the myth came from

It survives for a reason, and the reason is worth understanding because it explains what people are actually noticing.

Differences are salient. The things you share with your partner are invisible — you do not spend evenings marvelling at your shared assumption that people should be on time. The things you differ on generate stories, and the stories get told. So couples describe themselves as opposites while quietly agreeing on almost everything that structures their life together.

There is also a real phenomenon underneath it, just not the one advertised. Complementarity in roles — one person handling the logistics, the other the social calendar — is common, useful, and quite different from being opposite in values. Sharing a worldview while dividing the labour is not the myth. It is closer to how it actually works.

What is worth taking from it

Stop diagnosing incompatibility from surface differences. Being an introvert with an extrovert is not a structural flaw, and it is not what the data points to as consequential. Values, and whether you want the same shape of life, do far more work.

Take the boring agreements seriously. The strongest similarities in the data were things like politics, religion and education — the substrate people rarely discuss in early dating because it seems less interesting than chemistry. It is not less interesting. It is what you will be living inside.

Do not romanticise friction as proof of depth. There is a story people tell in difficult relationships — that the intensity of the conflict demonstrates how significant the connection is. The evidence gives no support to the idea that being unlike each other is an asset, and the belief that it is can keep people in something for years on the grounds that it was always supposed to be hard.

And notice which differences are actually live. Most couples have two or three real ones, not a general incompatibility. Naming them specifically turns a vague sense of mismatch into something you can actually have a conversation about — which is a conversation worth preparing.

The part that gets left out

There is a slightly bleak corollary in this research that is worth stating, because it is more socially important than the dating advice.

If people reliably partner with those similar to them in education, politics and income, then couples are a mechanism through which those things concentrate rather than mix — across households, and over generations. The researchers were explicit that the findings should not be misused to push an agenda, and that caution is well placed. But the pattern is there, and it is a bigger deal at the level of a society than at the level of your relationship.

For your relationship, the practical message is smaller and more reassuring: you are probably more alike than the stories you tell about yourselves suggest, and the differences you argue about are unlikely to be the ones that determine anything.
