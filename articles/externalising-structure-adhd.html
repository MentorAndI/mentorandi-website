Externalising structure: put it outside your head, where it can't quietly disappear

If holding the plan in mind is the part that keeps failing, the fix is not a better memory. It is to stop requiring one.

From ADHD Mentor·8 min read

Most productivity advice quietly assumes an internal manager. Something in you that holds the day's shape, notices when you have drifted, remembers what you decided this morning, and steps in with a correction. Nearly every system on the market is a set of instructions for that manager.

If the manager is the part that struggles, the instructions do not help. You end up with an elaborate system that requires exactly the capacity it was meant to compensate for — which is why so many people have a graveyard of abandoned apps and a nagging sense that they broke something that works fine for everyone else.

Externalising structure starts from the opposite assumption. Rather than trying to strengthen the internal manager, you move its jobs out into the physical world, where they persist without being held.

Why this is the right lever

Barkley's model of ADHD, the most cited framework in the field, puts behavioural inhibition at the centre and describes several executive functions as depending on it — among them working memory, the self-regulation of motivation, and the internal speech you use to instruct yourself through a sequence.

Notice what all three have in common: they are internal representations doing work over time. Holding the goal while you do something else. Generating urgency for a deadline that is not here yet. Narrating the next step to yourself.

An external structure replaces each of those with something that does not need to be held. The goal is on the wall. The urgency comes from a timer that is visibly running. The sequence is written down, so there is nothing to narrate.

There is direct experimental support for the cueing part specifically. Gawrilow and Gollwitzer found that when children with ADHD converted a goal into an if-then plan — a specific cue tied to a specific response — their performance inhibiting an unwanted response on a Go/No-Go task improved to the level of children without ADHD. The intention was the same in both conditions. What changed was that it had been attached to a trigger rather than left as a general aim.

You are not building a system to follow. You are building conditions where the right thing happens without a decision being required.

The four things worth externalising

Time

Time is the first thing to leave the head, because it is the least reliable thing in there. "Later" and "soon" and "after this" are not durations, and an hour spent absorbed feels nothing like an hour spent waiting.

The fix is to make time visible rather than known. A clock tells you what time it is; it does not show you time passing. Something that displays a shrinking amount — a visual timer, a progress bar, a physical hourglass — converts an abstraction into something you can see out of the corner of your eye. Most people find this uncomfortable at first, which is the point: it is doing the job your internal sense of elapsed time was not.

The next action

A task list is usually a list of projects wearing task clothing. "Sort out insurance" is not an action; it is a topic, and it will sit there for a fortnight because there is no visible thing to begin.

Externalising means writing down the physical first move. Not "sort out insurance" but "open the app and find the renewal date". The test is whether someone else could perform the item without asking you a question. If they could not, it is not yet a next action, and your brain will keep bouncing off it.

Cues

An intention with no trigger relies on you spontaneously remembering at the right moment — the exact thing that fails. A cue moves the remembering into the environment.

The structure is when X happens, I do Y, and it is the same mechanism that makes habit stacking work. The strongest cues are things that already happen without fail and are hard to walk past: the kettle going on, sitting down at the desk, the front door closing behind you. Weak cues are times of day, moods, and any sentence containing the word "whenever".

State

The last one is the least obvious and often the most useful. Write down where you left off, before you stop.

Half the cost of an interrupted task is not the interruption — it is the twenty minutes of reconstruction when you come back and cannot find your place. A single line at the end of a session ("stopped mid-paragraph three, next: rewrite the opening") removes the re-entry cost almost entirely, and re-entry cost is one of the main reasons things get abandoned rather than finished.

How to build one without it collapsing

The failure mode is over-construction. Someone reads a piece like this, spends a Sunday building a beautiful system with eleven components, uses it for four days and then never again — and files it as another personal failure rather than what it actually is, which is a design that needed more upkeep than it saved.

Some rules that hold up.

One structure at a time. Externalise the single thing that broke most recently, not everything you can imagine breaking. Add the next only when the first has stopped requiring thought.

Prefer physical to digital where you can. A whiteboard cannot be closed, minimised, or buried under sixteen tabs. Anything living behind a notification is competing with everything else behind a notification, and it usually loses.

Put it where the action happens. A list in an app you have to remember to open has reintroduced the exact problem you were solving. The note goes on the door, the timer goes on the desk, the folder goes on the chair by the keys.

Assume it will decay. Every external structure quietly stops working — you stop seeing the sign, the whiteboard becomes wallpaper. This is normal and it is not a relapse. Move it, rewrite it, change the colour. Decay is maintenance, not failure.

The honest limit

This will not make the underlying difficulty disappear, and anyone promising that is selling something.

What it does is stop you paying, repeatedly and invisibly, for the one thing that is hardest to supply on demand. That is a smaller claim than most productivity writing makes and a much more reliable one — and, over a year, it is the difference between a life that runs on heroic recovery and one that mostly just runs.
