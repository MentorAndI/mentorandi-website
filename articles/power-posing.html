Power posing: what happened after the TED talk

Two minutes in a superhero stance, and your hormones shift. It became one of the most watched talks ever given. Then the first author of the study said she no longer believed it.

From Charisma Mentor·8 min read

If you have ever been told to stand in a toilet cubicle with your hands on your hips before a job interview, this is where that came from — and it is worth knowing how the story ended, because almost nobody who repeats the advice has heard the second half.

The original claim

In 2010, Dana Carney, Amy Cuddy and Andy Yap published a study in Psychological Science. Participants held either expansive, open postures or contracted, closed ones for a couple of minutes. The paper reported that the expansive poses produced a rise in testosterone, a drop in cortisol, greater tolerance for risk, and a stronger felt sense of power.

It was an irresistible finding. A free, instant, physiological intervention requiring nothing but your own body. The TED talk that followed became one of the most viewed of all time, and the advice spread into interview coaching, sales training and school assemblies.

What happened next

In 2015, Ranehill and colleagues ran a considerably larger replication. The headline claims did not hold up: the hormonal and behavioural effects failed to appear.

Then, in 2016, something unusual happened. Dana Carney — first author of the original paper — published a statement saying plainly that she does not believe power pose effects are real. She went further, describing methodological problems with the original study, including analytic choices of the kind now called p-hacking: the flexible, after-the-fact decisions that can turn noise into a publishable result without anyone intending to deceive.

It is rare for a researcher to take apart their own most famous finding in public. It was also, by most accounts, the right thing to do.

The person best placed to defend the study read the accumulated evidence and concluded against it. That is not a scandal. It is the system working, slowly and expensively.

What is still argued about

It would be unfair to say nothing survived, and the honest version is more interesting than a clean debunk.

The claims that collapsed are the physiological and behavioural ones — the hormones, the risk-taking, the idea that a posture rewires your body chemistry before a meeting. Those are the claims that made the finding famous, and they are the ones you should stop repeating.

What remains disputed is a much narrower claim: that adopting an expansive posture makes people report feeling somewhat more powerful. Amy Cuddy has continued to argue for that reduced version, and the debate has not fully closed.

Notice how much smaller that is. "You may briefly report feeling a bit more confident" is a very different product from "your testosterone rises and you make better decisions." One is a modest self-report effect. The other was a promise about your endocrine system.

What this should change about how you prepare

Stop treating posture as a pre-match ritual. Two minutes in a cubicle is not going to alter how the interview goes. If it settles your nerves, fine — but bank it as a small comfort, not a performance intervention, and do not skip the preparation that actually moves the outcome.

Separate how you feel from how you read. There is a second, quite different question the power-posing debate obscured: how your posture affects what other people conclude about you. Slumping, shrinking and hiding your hands do change the impression you make. That is worth working on — but it is about the observer, not about your hormones, and it is trained by feedback rather than by two minutes alone in a lift.

Aim at the things with better evidence. If you have ten minutes before something that matters, they are better spent on your opening line, the question you plan to ask, or the single point you want them to remember. Those have a much stronger research base than anything you can do with your elbows.

The wider lesson, which is the useful one

This case is the reason we do not quote you confident percentages from single small studies on this site.

The original power-posing paper had 42 participants. It was published in a good journal, written by capable researchers, and it was wrong — not through fraud, but through the ordinary combination of a small sample, flexible analysis and a result too good to interrogate hard. It then travelled to tens of millions of people before anyone checked.

When you next meet a communication technique attached to a striking number, the useful questions are: how many people, has anyone independently repeated it, and what do the original authors say now. Most popular advice in this field cannot answer all three.
