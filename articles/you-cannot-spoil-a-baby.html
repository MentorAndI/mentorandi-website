You cannot spoil a baby by picking them up

Somebody has told you that you are making a rod for your own back. It is worth knowing exactly where that advice came from, because it did not come from evidence.

From Parenting Mentor·7 min read

It usually arrives from a relative, kindly meant, at about week three. You're holding him too much. You'll make a rod for your own back. He needs to learn to settle himself.

It lands hard, because at three weeks you have no idea what you are doing and everyone sounds more confident than you. So it is worth knowing where that particular piece of advice actually originates.

It comes from a 1928 book

The idea that affection spoils an infant is not folk wisdom passed down through centuries of grandmothers. It was, to a significant degree, a specific twentieth-century programme, and its most influential exponent was the American behaviourist John B. Watson.

His 1928 manual on the psychological care of infant and child advised parents against the ordinary demonstrations of affection — no hugging and kissing, no lap-sitting, a handshake in the morning. The reasoning was that sentimentality made children dependent and unfit for the world, and it was delivered with the full authority of the new science of behaviour.

It was enormously influential. It shaped a generation of childcare advice, hospital policy and grandmothers. And it rested on a theory about conditioning, not on any evidence that responsive parents produced clingy children.

Nobody ever demonstrated that comforting a baby harms them. The claim arrived as an assertion, and it has been repeated ever since as though it had been tested.

What the research actually looked at

The best-known study on the other side is Bell and Ainsworth's, published in Child Development in 1972. It followed twenty-six mother–infant pairs naturalistically over the first year, and reported that mothers who responded promptly and consistently to crying had infants who, later in the year, cried less and communicated more in other ways.

Their conclusion was the direct reverse of the spoiling thesis: prompt response was associated not with dependence but with a healthier balance between attachment and going off to explore.

Now the honest part, because this article would be worse without it.

That specific finding has been contested. A critical replication argued that the correlations on duration measures were spurious — that once you control for other things influencing how much a baby cries in the first place, the pattern does not hold up as cleanly. A separate, larger replication of the Baltimore work, observing fifty families for more than twenty hours in their homes, found descriptive data remarkably similar to the original.

So: the claim that responsiveness reduces crying is genuinely debated. It is a smaller sample and a harder measurement problem than the confident version you will read elsewhere.

Which does not rescue the spoiling myth at all

Because notice what is being argued about. The dispute is over whether responsiveness produces measurably less crying.

Nobody in that debate is arguing the original claim — that responding to your baby causes harm, dependency or a rod for anybody's back. That claim has never had support. It arrived as a theory in the 1920s, was applied to millions of children, and the evidence assembled since has consistently pointed the other way or, at worst, been equivocal about the size of the benefit.

The burden of proof was always on the person telling you to leave the baby. It has never been met.

What this means at three in the morning

Pick them up. There is no version of the evidence in which comforting a distressed infant is the risky choice.

Understand what crying is. In the early months it is not manipulation, because the equipment for manipulation has not arrived yet. It is the only signal available. Responding to a signal teaches that signals work, which is the foundation of every later form of communication.

Separate infants from older children. Almost all the confusion comes from collapsing these. Whether a seven-year-old should always get what they want the moment they complain is a real question with real answers on both sides. Whether a seven-week-old can be spoiled by being held is not the same question, and the answer is no.

And know that you cannot respond every time. You will be in the shower, driving, or holding another child. Perfect responsiveness is not the standard and never was — which is a point worth taking seriously in its own right, because the pursuit of it does measurable harm to parents.

Why this myth is worth killing specifically

Because of who it hurts. It arrives at the exact moment when new parents have the least confidence and the most exhaustion, and it tells them that their instinct — pick up the crying baby — is a weakness to be resisted.

It sets people against their own judgement in the first month of their child's life, on the authority of a book from 1928 that almost nobody quoting it has read.

This is general reflection on everyday family life. It is not medical advice, not a substitute for your health visitor, midwife, GP or paediatrician, and an AI mentor does not diagnose or treat anything. If your baby's crying is inconsolable, unusual for them, or accompanied by illness, contact a medical professional. If you are struggling with your own mood after birth, please tell your GP or health visitor — postnatal depression is common and treatable. If you are in crisis, contact your local emergency services or a crisis line in your country.
