Telling your child they're clever is the risky compliment

It is the most natural thing to say when they bring home a good mark. In six studies it produced children who gave up faster — and the finding is narrower than you have been told.

From Parenting Mentor·8 min read

They come in with the test back, pleased, and hand it over. You say the obvious thing: you're so clever.

It is warm, it is meant, and it is the single most examined sentence in the parenting literature — because in a set of experiments from 1998, it reliably made children worse at handling the next difficult thing.

The study

Claudia Mueller and Carol Dweck ran six studies with fifth-graders, published in the Journal of Personality and Social Psychology.

Children worked on a set of problems, did well, and were praised. Some were praised for intelligence — you must be smart at these. Others were praised for effort — you must have worked hard at these. One sentence, one difference.

Then they were given a much harder set, and most of them struggled.

The children praised for intelligence responded worse on nearly every measure. They persisted less, enjoyed the task less, attributed their failure to not being clever enough, and performed worse on a final set that was back at the original difficulty. They also cared more about looking good than about learning — when offered a choice, they leaned towards tasks that would let them perform well rather than tasks they would learn from.

The praise did not make them proud. It told them what was being measured — and after that, every hard problem was a test of whether it was still true.

Why one sentence does that

Because of what it identifies as the cause.

"You're clever" locates the success in a fixed property of the child. That is lovely while things go well. The moment something is hard, the same logic runs in reverse: if success meant I am clever, then struggling means I am not. The rational move at that point is to stop — because continuing risks producing more evidence against you.

"You worked hard at that" locates the success in something the child did, which is available again next time. Difficulty then means the thing needs more work, not that you have been found out.

This is also why bright children are so often the ones who fall apart at the first genuinely hard subject. They have had years of being told what they are, and no practice at being someone who finds something difficult.

The honest limits

Here we have to be careful, because this research sits next to a much bigger and much shakier body of work.

Growth mindset — the broader theory these studies belong to — has had a difficult decade. Large school interventions designed to teach children that ability is malleable have produced mixed meta-analyses, small effects and real concerns about publication bias. If you have seen claims that a short mindset workshop transforms academic results, treat them with suspicion.

The praise findings specifically have held up better than the interventions. But they are not universal either: attempts to reproduce the effect in other populations have sometimes found nothing — one study following the original procedure with vocational education students found the type of praise made no difference to them.

So the defensible claim is narrower than the internet version. It is not "praise for intelligence damages children". It is: with children of around this age, in this kind of task, praising ability rather than effort made them less resilient to a subsequent failure. That is worth acting on. It is not worth panicking about.

What to say instead

Describe what you actually saw. "You stuck with that even when it got annoying." "You tried three different ways before it worked." Specific, observed, and about something they did. This is the whole technique, and it takes practice because the generic version is so much faster to say.

Praise the strategy, not just the graft. "You worked hard" is better than "you're clever", but it has a failure mode: if a child works hard and still fails, effort praise leaves them nowhere. Better is the method — what they tried, how they got unstuck, what they did differently. That gives them something to change.

Be careful with easy wins. When something was genuinely effortless, "you worked so hard" is a lie and children detect it instantly. Say something true instead: that was quick, shall we find you something harder. The point is not to manufacture effort talk; it is to keep the focus off fixed ability.

Watch what you say after failure, not just after success. This is where most of it is decided. "Never mind, you're not a maths person" is intelligence praise wearing a comforting expression, and it does the same damage in one sentence.

And say the loving things anyway. None of this means you cannot tell your child they are wonderful. Warmth is not a technique and does not need to be optimised. The distinction is between adoring your child, which they need, and explaining their achievements in terms of a fixed trait, which is the specific thing under discussion.

The one to actually take away

If you remember nothing else: praise what they did, not what they are.

And do not treat this as a rule you must police perfectly. You will say "you're so clever" this week — everyone does. It is the pattern across years that shapes what a child believes about difficulty, not any one sentence at the kitchen table. Which is also why guilt is a poor tool here, and why the pursuit of getting everything right has costs of its own.
