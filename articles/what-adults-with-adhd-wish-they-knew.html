What adults with ADHD wish they'd worked out sooner

Researchers have spent years asking the same question in interview rooms. The answers repeat — and almost everyone arrives at them the slow way, on their own.

From ADHD Mentor·8 min read

There is a particular kind of knowledge that does not appear in the leaflets. It gets assembled slowly, usually after several failed attempts at doing things the way everyone else appears to, and it tends to arrive about a decade later than it would have been useful.

The interesting thing is that it is not idiosyncratic. When researchers sit down with adults with ADHD and ask what they have learned, the same things keep surfacing — across countries, across studies, across people who have never met.

A systematic review by Bjerrum, Pedersen and Larsen pulled together 103 findings from ten qualitative studies and condensed them into four themes. A separate Swiss study interviewed thirty-two adults specifically about the strategies they had built for themselves, and sorted what they described into categories.

What follows is drawn from those. Not advice invented for an article — a summary of what people who have lived it say they worked out.

That the strategies would have to be self-built

The clearest finding in the Swiss interviews is that people had assembled their own toolkits, and that the tools clustered into recognisable kinds: organisational ones — checklists, reminders, rigid daily structures held in place against forgetting; attentional ones — deliberately choosing low-stimulus environments, working at night, cutting down on interruption; physical ones — using movement to settle, or restricting it to hold focus; and social ones — protecting punctuality, avoiding commitments that could not be kept.

Two things are worth noticing about that list.

The first is how physical it is. Almost nothing in it is about attitude or resolve. It is about arranging rooms, hours and objects — the same logic as externalising structure, arrived at independently by people who had never read a paper on it.

The second is that it was self-built, largely by trial and error, over years. Which raises the obvious question of how much of that decade might have been saved.

Nearly everyone eventually builds a version of the same toolkit. Almost nobody is handed one.

That the environment is a variable, not a given

The single most repeated practical insight is that where and when you work is not a fixed condition you must cope with. It is an input you can change, and changing it often does more than any amount of effort applied inside a bad setting.

People described seeking out quiet, unstimulating places. Working when the world was asleep. Reducing the number of things capable of interrupting them, rather than trying to get better at resisting interruption.

This sounds obvious written down. It is not obvious when you have spent years being told the problem is your concentration, because that framing makes the room seem irrelevant. A great many people spend a long time trying to become the sort of person who can work in an open-plan office before it occurs to them that they could simply not do that.

That organising everyday life is genuinely hard — and that this is not a private failing

One of the review's four themes is that accomplishing and organising ordinary tasks is a real challenge, and also that it can be rewarding when it works.

Both halves matter. The first half is permission to stop treating the difficulty as a secret defect — it is the common experience, described consistently by people who do not know each other. The second half is the part that gets lost in gloomier accounts: when a structure does hold, people report it as genuinely satisfying rather than merely a relief.

That combination argues against two popular framings at once. It is not trivial, and it is not tragic. It is a real difficulty with a real payoff for working on it.

That being different was the thing taking up the most energy

The review's first theme is about awareness of being different from others, and the effort of trying to be an accepted part of things.

This is the least practical item here and probably the heaviest. A great deal of energy goes not into the tasks themselves but into the management of appearances around them — concealing how much effort something took, pre-emptively apologising, avoiding situations where the difference might show.

What people report, generally later than they would have liked, is how much of that overhead was optional. Not all of it — the world is not uniformly kind about this. But a meaningful portion turns out to be self-imposed, and the cost of carrying it was often larger than the cost of the original difficulty.

The one to be careful with

The review also reports a theme of creativity and inventiveness, and it deserves handling with care.

You will have seen the internet version: ADHD as a superpower, a hunter in a farmer's world, a gift misunderstood. That framing is generally unhelpful. It quietly implies that anyone struggling is failing to use their gift properly, which adds a fresh layer of blame to an existing difficulty.

What the review actually reports is more modest and more credible: this is a theme in how adults describe their own experience. It is qualitative — people's accounts of themselves, synthesised — not a measured comparison of creative output between groups. Read it as "many people experience their thinking as inventive, and that is worth taking seriously," not as a compensating superpower that cancels out the hard parts.

The thing under all of it

Read those findings together and a pattern shows through. Almost every useful thing on the list was worked out alone, late, and expensively.

Not because the information is secret. Because knowing a technique is not the same as knowing which technique applies to you, in your job, in your flat, on a Wednesday in February when the last three things you tried have lapsed. That knowledge only comes from someone tracking what you actually tried and what happened — which, for most people, has been nobody.

That is the gap worth closing, and it is closable much earlier than most people manage.

This piece summarises published qualitative research about everyday experience and self-built strategies. It is not diagnostic and not clinical advice.
