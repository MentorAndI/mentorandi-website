They liked you more than you think. This is measurable.

The post-conversation replay, where you count everything you said wrong, is running on bad data. Researchers have measured how bad, and given it a name.

From Charisma Mentor·7 min read

You leave the conversation. Somewhere between the door and the car, the review begins. That story went on too long. You interrupted them once. There was a pause you filled badly, and a joke that landed at about half the height you had planned.

Meanwhile the other person is walking in the opposite direction thinking, if they are thinking about it at all, that was nice.

This is not reassurance. It is a measured, repeatable finding with a name.

The liking gap

Boothby, Cooney, Sandstrom and Clark published the work in Psychological Science in 2018. The design is simple: have two people talk, then ask each of them privately how much they liked the other and how much they think the other liked them. Then compare.

People systematically underestimated how much their conversation partners liked them and enjoyed their company. The researchers called it the liking gap.

What makes the finding hard to dismiss is where they found it. Not just among strangers in a laboratory, but among first-year students getting to know their dorm mates over an extended period, and among members of the public getting acquainted at a personal development workshop. Different settings, different lengths of acquaintance, same gap.

Both people walk away thinking they were the one who was tolerated. Statistically, at least one of them is wrong.

Why it happens

The researchers' explanation is unglamorous and rings true: in a new conversation people are self-protective and self-focused. Your inner commentary is running on you — monitoring your performance, cataloguing your errors, wondering how you are coming across.

You are, in other words, the harshest critic in the room, and you are the only critic with access to the full transcript of what you nearly said, what you meant to say, and what you were worried about while saying it. The other person has none of that. They heard a person talking, at a normal volume, saying reasonable things.

And crucially, they were busy doing exactly the same thing about themselves.

Why this matters more than it sounds

It would be easy to file this as a nice fact and move on. But it has consequences that quietly shape a life.

It makes you stop too early. If you believe a conversation went badly, you do not follow up. You do not suggest the coffee, send the message, go to the second event. The relationship that would have formed does not, and the belief that you are bad at this is confirmed by an absence you created.

It makes practice feel like failure. Anyone training a social skill judges progress by how the conversation felt. If your internal read is systematically pessimistic, you will conclude that a drill isn't working while it is working — and abandon it.

It hides your actual weak points. When everything registers as "that went badly", you get no signal about which parts genuinely need work. Undifferentiated dread is useless feedback.

What to do with it

Adjust the estimate, deliberately. Not "it went brilliantly" — just: my read is biased downwards, so the truth is probably somewhere better than this feels. That is not positive thinking, it is correcting a known measurement error.

Act on the corrected number. The point of knowing this is behavioural. Send the follow-up message you were not going to send. Suggest the second meeting. You are not being pushy; you are compensating for an instrument that reads low.

Get external data. Your internal read is the one thing you should trust least. Whether people accept your invitations, reply, and seek you out again is far more informative than how the conversation felt at minute forty.

Point attention outwards. The gap is driven by self-focus, so the practical antidote is attending to them rather than to yourself — which is exactly what asking follow-up questions forces you to do. It improves how you come across and shrinks the replay at the same time.

The limit worth stating

This does not mean every conversation went well. Some genuinely do not, and there are people who are disliked for reasons they could address.

What the evidence supports is narrower and still useful: your estimate is biased in a known direction, and the confident feeling that you were merely tolerated is not evidence. If you have been making decisions about whether to pursue friendships, jobs or relationships based on that feeling, you have been using an instrument that reads low — and you can correct for it starting with the next conversation.

This is general reflection on everyday social life. An AI mentor teaches presence, voice and conversation — it is not a therapist and does not diagnose or treat social anxiety or any other condition. If replaying conversations, dreading them, or avoiding people is significantly affecting your life, that deserves a qualified professional rather than a training drill.
