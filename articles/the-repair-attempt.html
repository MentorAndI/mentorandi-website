The repair attempt: the small move that stops a row escalating

A daft voice, a hand on an arm, "sorry, that came out wrong". Small, unimpressive, and quite possibly the most useful thing in the whole conflict literature.

From Relationship Mentor·7 min read

Watch a couple who are good at this and you will see it, though you would not notice unless you were looking.

The argument is heating. Voices are climbing. And then one of them does something faintly ridiculous — puts on a silly voice, says "okay, I'm being a nightmare", offers a biscuit mid-sentence, reaches over and takes the other's hand while still mid-complaint. Something in the room drops half a degree. The conversation continues, but the trajectory has changed.

Gottman gave that move a name: a repair attempt — in his definition, any statement or action, silly or otherwise, that prevents negativity from escalating out of control.

What it is, and what it is not

It is not resolution. Nothing has been agreed, nobody has conceded, and the disagreement is exactly where it was. That is the point: a repair attempt operates on the temperature, not on the substance.

It is also not politeness. Repair attempts are frequently graceless. "Can we start this again, I'm being horrible" is not elegant, and it works better than a well-phrased continuation of the row.

And crucially, it is not one-sided. The move only functions if the other person lets it — which means there are two skills here, and the second one is harder. Making a repair attempt takes a bit of humility. Accepting one, when you are angry and right, takes considerably more, because accepting it means giving up the momentum you currently have.

The row does not end because someone wins. It ends because someone offers a way down, and the other person takes it.

What the evidence actually supports — and what it doesn't

Here we should be straight with you, because this is a case where the popular version outruns what can be verified.

The concept comes from Gottman's observational work with couples, and it is genuinely influential — it is taught widely in couples therapy and it maps onto something most people recognise immediately from their own relationships. That recognisability is worth something, though it is not evidence.

What is harder to find is strong independent verification. A great deal of what circulates about repair attempts — the confident claims about how strongly they predict whether a relationship lasts — traces back to the Gottman Institute's own materials rather than to replication by unaffiliated researchers. And the broader body of divorce-prediction work this sits within has attracted serious methodological criticism: models fitted to known outcomes are not the same as predictions validated on new couples.

So we are not going to quote you a percentage. What we can say is narrower and still worth acting on: de-escalating a conflict before it spirals is plainly better than not doing so, the observational finding that couples differ in whether they de-escalate is well described, and the move costs nothing to try.

Treat it as a good idea with clinical pedigree rather than a proven mechanism. That is an unglamorous description, and it is the accurate one.

What they look like

They fall roughly into two kinds.

Some are about the process — comments on the argument rather than in it. "We're going round in circles." "I don't want to fight about this." "Can I start that again?" "Let me try to say that better." These work by stepping outside the row for a second, which is often enough to break the rhythm.

Others are about connection, and they frequently are not verbal at all. Touching an arm. Making eye contact instead of looking away. Using a private joke. Softening your face. These do not address the argument at all; they remind both people that the other one is not actually the enemy.

What almost never works is anything that smuggles the argument inside the repair. "I'm sorry you feel that way" is not a repair attempt. "Fine, whatever, let's just drop it" is not one either. Both are moves in the fight wearing a peace flag, and both are detected instantly.

How to use it

Agree on one in advance. This sounds contrived and works remarkably well. A specific phrase — anything, as long as it is yours — that either of you can say to mean "I want out of this spiral, not out of this conversation." Agreeing it while calm removes the need to invent it while angry, which is when nobody invents anything.

Make it early. Repair attempts are much easier to accept before either person is fully escalated. Once someone is genuinely flooded, almost nothing lands, and the honest move at that point is a pause rather than a repair.

Practise accepting. If your partner offers a clumsy one — a bad joke at a tense moment — and you refuse it because it was not good enough, you have taught them that trying is risky. Taking a poor repair attempt seriously is one of the more generous things available in a relationship, and it costs about two seconds.

Do not use it to avoid the conversation. The technique de-escalates; it does not resolve. If every difficult subject gets defused and then never returns, you have not repaired anything, you have postponed it — and the postponed version comes back louder. Lower the temperature, then come back to it properly.

Why it matters more than the argument itself

Most couples do not have many disagreements. They have a few, which recur.

Which means the question is not really how to resolve the argument — most of these are not resolvable in any final sense. The question is whether you can have it, repeatedly, over years, without doing damage along the way.

That is what this technique is for. Not winning, not solving. Making a recurring disagreement survivable, so that having it for the ninth time costs you very little.

This is general guidance for everyday relationships. It is not therapy, not clinical advice and not a substitute for couples counselling. An AI mentor does not diagnose or treat anything. De-escalation techniques are not appropriate where a relationship involves control, intimidation, threats or any form of abuse — in that situation please contact a domestic abuse service or your local emergency services rather than trying to manage it yourself.
